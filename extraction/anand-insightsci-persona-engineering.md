| `el-synthetic-persona` | Synthetic persona | concept | context | An LLM steered to respond as a particular person or segment and then used as a **survey respondent** — companies test product concepts and messaging against panels of them. Grew directly out of ordinary role prompting ("you are a ___, now do this task") into a funded category with visible market momentum (headlines plus rising funding over recent years). The talk's governing frame: a synthetic persona is a **forecast, not a person** — like weather forecasting it was unlocked by compute plus data, it operates inside a regime beyond which it stops being accurate, and it is trustworthy only where validated against reality |
| `el-latent-confounder-invention` | Latent-confounder invention | concept | context | The first and most fundamental persona failure mode: **when context is missing, the model invents it**, so parts of the experiment that should be held fixed silently become random variables. Documented case: asked about willingness to purchase at varying prices, humans slope downward as basic economic theory predicts, while LLMs produce an **inverted-U** — purchase probability *rising* with price over part of the range — because the model reads price as a proxy for unstated properties (expiry date, competitors' prices) that human respondents held fixed. "If it's a poorly grounded persona, it's a little like the LLM is playing improv with you — gold watch on a table? We must be in a jewelry store." The fix inverts human-subject practice: paint the whole world in the prompt, **including the study's own construction**, which you would deliberately hide from a human participant |
| `el-persona-durability-testing` | Persona durability testing | concept | context | Stress-testing a persona for prompt sensitivity before trusting its answers, because sensitivity can exceed signal: in one study the same question with the same options but the **order of the choices swapped** produced such a strong order bias that averaging the two runs washed out to 50/50 noise. Humans have a first-order bias too, "but not to this extent". The discipline: test personas under reorderings, rewordings, and adversarial challenges to their stated opinions, and treat instability as a property of the instrument rather than of the respondent |
| `el-attitude-behavior-gap` | Attitude/behavior gap | concept | context | LLMs are trained on **what people say, not what people do**, so stated attitudes are easier to predict than actions. Evidence: across replicated social-science studies an LLM matched expert forecasters overall, but the two study families separate cleanly — **surveys** (natively language, measuring attitudes) are predicted well, **field experiments** (behaviors, which must be transcribed into text before they can enter training data) noticeably worse. Practical rule given to clients: build questions that **triangulate to behavior from attitudes** — to learn about gym attendance, ask about attitudes toward working out and test whether that proxies |
| `el-semantic-anchor-scoring` | Semantic-anchor scoring | concept | harness | Elicitation technique that recovers distributions instead of point estimates. Rather than forcing a persona onto a 1–5 purchase-intent scale (a format LLMs aren't used to producing), let it answer in **free text** — its native format — e.g. "I'm somewhat interested. If it works well and isn't too expensive, I might give it a try." Then have humans write anchor texts for each scale point ("Hell no, I'll never buy that" … "Absolutely, I'll buy 20"), measure **semantic similarity** between the model's text and the anchors, and read off a probability distribution over the scale. Result reported: the technique reconstructed not only accurate willingness-to-pay values but the **shape** of the distribution, which naive rating prompts flatten |
| `el-subpop-finetuning` | Subpop-style distribution fine-tuning | technology | training | Fine-tuning a model against **known human response distributions**: prompt template = demographic information + the survey question; compare the model's output distribution to the real human distribution; fine-tune until they align. The notable result is transfer — alignment improved on the populations supplied (expected) **and nearly as much on unseen groups** (not). Interpretation offered, with subsequent research cited as support: the model already holds a latent understanding of these groups and simply didn't know how to express it in survey format, so fine-tuning taught it the **task**, not the content. "Your persona is in there; we just need to figure out how to summon it." ⚠ paper name from captions ("the Subpop paper") |
| `el-human-noise-floor` | Human noise floor | concept | harness | Measurement discipline that sets the ceiling on any human-alignment score. In the field's best-known demonstration (~1,000 people interviewed for ~2.5 hours each, then put through personality tests and surveys; transcripts handed to agents that took the same battery), the headline **83% agent-human alignment is normalized** against the humans' own consistency — the same people were brought back two weeks later and were only **~80% self-consistent**. Where re-testing humans isn't possible, approximate it: split the human ground-truth data in half, treat one half as "synthetic", measure the correlation, and repeat hundreds or thousands of times to estimate the floor. Corollary: because a persona is a forecast, **re-running it does not buy certainty** — a thousand rain gauges improve an estimate of today's rainfall, a thousand reruns of tomorrow's forecast improve only your estimate of what the model says |
| `el-distribution-alignment-metrics` | Distribution-alignment metrics | concept | harness | Evaluating personas as a **comparison of distributions**, not as right/wrong scoring: "unlike classic evals where there's clearly a right and wrong and you can score how many were right, now we need to measure the data as a comparison of distributions." Distributions fail in more than one way — wildly off, or (the common and dangerous case) **average right and shape wrong**, with the variation muddled into the middle — so use multiple metrics: a correlation-type metric plus a distribution-shape metric. Also: more synthetic samples do not confer statistical significance, so an underrepresented population can be extended but not made significant |
| `el-generative-agent-based-modeling` | Generative agent-based modeling | concept | context | Named forward direction: rather than querying personas independently, **simulate a population of them interacting** — dynamics between personas rather than isolated responses — as the path to turning static human research data into "a living, queryable asset". ⚠ thin: named and framed in the closing minute, with "we didn't get time for this"; coined because it is the field's stated next step and connects the persona cluster to the corpus's simulation material |

## Review notes

1. **`pat-benchmark-trust-crisis` — ⚑ a genuinely NEW leg: ground-truth noise (NOT coined, per instructions).** The four existing legs (gaming, contamination, methodology, economic motive) all concern the *instrument*. Anand supplies the leg about the *target*: **the humans a benchmark is validated against are only ~80% consistent with themselves at two weeks**, which sets a hard ceiling on any human-alignment score and makes an unnormalized percentage meaningless. The field's best-known persona result is 83% only because it was explicitly normalized against that floor; almost nothing else in the corpus's benchmark material reports a noise floor at all. He adds the companion claim that for anything human-like, "classic evals where there's clearly a right and wrong" are the wrong instrument entirely — you need distribution comparison, and distributions fail in ways a score cannot show (average right, shape wrong). If the pattern is coined, this leg is what distinguishes *benchmarks that are corrupted* from *benchmarks that are measuring an irreducibly noisy target and not saying so*. It also reframes batch-9's Thomas/Miranda evidence, which is the same claim about a different subject matter.
2. **`pat-agent-economy` — one signal held pattern-less (NOT coined, per instructions).** `sig-agent-mediated-consumers-break-human-ground-truth` carries the claim that "humans are no longer the sole economic actor" and that awareness, consideration and purchase are increasingly agent-mediated, so human-only market research no longer measures the deciding population. That is a distinctive addition to the candidate's ledger: Povilionis (receipts), Raskar (agentic web), Petersson (Vending-Bench agents hiring humans and forming cartels) and the Local-AI panel (agents provisioning compute) are all supply-side or agent-to-agent; this is the first **consumer-demand-side** data point — agents intermediating ordinary purchase decisions, with the practical consequence that a whole research industry's ground truth is going stale. Held pattern-less for clean rehoming; if you'd rather not carry a pattern-less signal, `pat-model-not-bottleneck` is the least-bad home.
3. **`el-miranda-hypothesis` gains its first *measured* corroboration — recommend keeping the link.** Batch 9 coined it from a documentary/archival argument (persona output as a salience-weighted composite of culturally dominant representations). That's the Miranda mechanism with a measurement attached, and it's the strongest argument in the corpus that persona fidelity is not a monotone function of specification. The edge is carried on `sig-persona-is-elicitation-not-knowledge` and `ins-persona-work-is-elicitation-engineering`; drop both if you read the two phenomena as unrelated (defensible — Miranda is about training-corpus composition, this is about prompt construction).

Merging them would collapse a subject/instrument distinction the corpus will want later; I linked them by reuse note rather than by edge.
