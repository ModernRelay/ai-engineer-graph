| `el-post-training-four-criteria` | The four criteria for post-training | concept | training | The evaluative frame that organizes the talk: a learning algorithm should have an **online task distribution** (tied to what users actually do, not a curated benchmark), **on-policy sampling**, **parallelism of one** (no fleet of rollouts, hence no environment infrastructure), and **per-token dense reward**. Applied historically: SFT gets parallelism and per-token reward but is off-policy on a curated distribution; DPO/RLHF gains online task distributions but reverts to sequence-level reward and pairwise infrastructure; **GRPO** is "a Faustian bargain" — maximal on-policy power at the cost of off-policy task distributions, exploded parallelism demanding one-to-one environment replicas, and whole-sequence reward |
| `el-on-policy-self-distillation` | On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) | technology | training | The proposed algorithm, derived in three steps. Classical distillation fits a student's log-probs to a *smarter teacher's* on fixed data; on-policy distillation swaps the fixed data for the student's own rollout; **self**-distillation resolves the remaining problem — at the frontier there is no smarter teacher — by making the teacher *the same model given privileged information*. A **hint** (in the simplest case a golden solution, more generally environment information or guidance) is prepended to the teacher's prompt, and the student without the hint is trained to match the teacher with it. Satisfies all four criteria at once. Because it matches the full distribution rather than sharpening a sampled token, it "shifts entire distributions" across a ~65k vocabulary per token rather than nudging the top choice |
| `el-hint-leakage` | Hint leakage | concept | training | OPSD's structural analogue of reward hacking, and the talk's most transferable warning: "with RL the number one problem is reward hacking… there is an equivalent for OPSD, and that is hint leakage." If the hint contains information the student could never have derived, the model learns to skip the reasoning that would have produced it — the worked example being a maths task where the hint states the answer and the rollout becomes "I know the solution is 000, so let me go back and put that into my reasoning trace." Two fixes offered: an LLM filter that rewrites hints into *what the model should reasonably have known* (e.g. "look through the logs" instead of "the SSO token is expired"), and the algorithmic **residual guidance** below |
| `el-step-level-divergence-weighting` | Step-level divergence weighting | technology | training | The fix for the long-horizon failure mode. On long tool-calling trajectories the student drifts far from the teacher, so the teacher course-corrects at every opportunity and the model collapses into hedging — the **"but/wait" problem**, visible as *wait*, *but* and *maybe* dominating a token cloud as steps progress, and as the model settling between two divergent distributions. The fix computes student-teacher KL divergence per step and uses it as a **multiplicative token weight** rather than as the usual KL penalty, so a diverged step is down-weighted while later steps that come back on track are still learned from |
| `el-residual-guidance` | Residual guidance | technology | training | The more principled anti-leakage method. Run a **partial** hint (e.g. half of it) to get a slightly-smarter teacher alongside the full-hint teacher, then train against a **linear combination** of the two. The combination measures how strong and how out-of-distribution the hint is, keeping the student from being shifted into territory with no overlap with its own distribution — the failure the talk illustrates as "entirely shifting distributions with almost no overlap between your model and the original solution" |
