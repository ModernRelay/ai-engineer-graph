## Elements (5 new)

| `el-atomic-provenance` | Atomic provenance | concept | data-eng | Kepler tenet 1. The model is never allowed to write or manipulate a number — it writes a **reference** to the number and hands off to a system built for numbers (a database). Every produced figure runs through a deterministic check against the provenance ledger; anything that can't be independently verified is **stripped out** and never reaches the user, and the cycle repeats at least twice. Explicitly *not* a multi-model cross-check ("these are not probabilistic systems evaluating each other's work") — the atomicity is database atomicity: extract, persist, confirm the process occurred. The ledger resolves a number to one of three origins: extraction from a filing, a derivative calculation, or the firm's internal documents |
| `el-scope-determinism` | Scope determinism | concept | harness | Kepler tenet 2. **The model decides what to compute; it never does the computation.** Reasoning and planning stay with the model ("intelligence is commoditized"); parsing a PDF or XBRL and running the arithmetic is code, and the deterministic layer persists the result outside anything the model can see. Rationale is both correctness and cost: "why would I run 1 + 1 through a multi-billion-parameter model instead of one CPU cycle?" Described as a contrarian position with VCs a year earlier that has since become mainstream |
| `el-derivation-chains` | Derivation chains | concept | data-eng | Kepler tenet 3. Reconciliation for figures that exist in no filing — gross margin, enterprise value, EBITDA adjustments, recurring-vs-non-recurring classification — where **every firm calculates differently**. The firm's conventions are codified into the processes that run, and a chain of events records what went into each individual number and each outcome, **replayable and rewindable**. Framed as the machine version of the chain of reasoning an analyst performs on a desk before a risk/reward decision |
| `el-verification-over-citation` | Verification over citation | concept | data-eng | The talk's core distinction. A **citation** shows where information came from and is "effectively an after-the-fact audit"; it got the industry "50% of the way there" and is undermined by uncurated sources (a random Substack, Seeking Alpha, Reddit are data points, not vetted sources). A **verification** is a deterministic, repeatable, numerically checkable mechanism proving a specific number is the correct one from a specific source. Corollary stated hard: **"evals are not verifiable — you cannot take a non-deterministic LLM and eval your way to something deterministic"**, and fine-tuning to 94% extraction accuracy is not a fix because "a wrong number is still wrong if you're in that unfortunate 6%" |
| `el-culpability-displacement` | Culpability displacement | concept | context | Why incumbent financial-data terminals are actually bought: not for information (much of it is free in SEC filings) but to **transfer blame** — a central vetted instance means "at least if it's wrong, everyone on Wall Street has the same incorrect information". The structural consequence is that every such tool is **read-only**: the analyst consumes and then produces the work product by hand, which is named as the biggest wall to meaningful AI adoption on Wall Street. Verifiable work product is the thing that dissolves the arrangement — a system that can prove its own numbers absorbs both the data-vetting contractors and the culpability |

| `how-split-the-model-from-the-math` | Let the model choose the computation, never perform it | Model the AI on a portfolio manager: it plans and decides inside a grounded scope with a risk threshold, and reaches for deterministic tools to act. Never let the model write or manipulate a number — have it emit a **reference** and hand off to a system built for numbers. Parse the PDF or the XBRL in code and persist the result outside the model's reach. Run every produced figure through an independent deterministic check and **strip anything that fails**, rather than surfacing it with a caveat. Do not build a probabilistic cross-check (one lab's model checking another's) — that is still probability checking probability. Repeat the extract → persist → confirm cycle at least twice. Side benefit: it is materially cheaper, because the model stops doing work it should never have been doing | `ReferencesElement → el-scope-determinism`, `el-atomic-provenance`, `el-deterministic-agentic-split` **[registry]** |
| `how-codify-firm-specific-derivations` | Codify the firm's conventions, then record the chain | Every derived figure that appears in no filing — gross margin, enterprise value, the EBITDA adjustment set, what counts as recurring — is calculated differently by every firm, so the firm's conventions must be codified into the processes that run, not inferred per query. Record a chain of events for each number and each outcome so both can be **replayed and rewound**, the way an analyst's reasoning on a desk can be walked back. Declare up front which data points the system is **allowed** to produce (structured filings, numerical data) and which it is **never** allowed to produce — if it would have to extract from prose or a raw table, it doesn't. Then every consolidated statement can be shipped with each number tied to its individual source, and the work product itself becomes the proof | `ReferencesElement → el-derivation-chains`, `el-atomic-provenance`, `el-verification-over-citation` |
