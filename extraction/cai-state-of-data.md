| `el-process-based-data` | Process-based data | concept | training | The trajectory rather than the artifact: the reasoning trace and the sequence of decisions that carry a professional from a blank page to a finished work output. | `el-type-one-type-two-data` | Type-1 vs type-2 data (non-contrived vs contrived) | concept | training | Quality axis sitting on top of process data. **Type 1** = pure capture of real workflows (GitHub commits, session replays) with minimal reward shaping by non-experts; realism is inherited from the work itself. **Type 2** = contrived: hire experts, sit them in an arbitrary setting, have them manufacture examples. Type 2 is the right place to start a model that is "reading at a first-grade level" — anyone can teach a third-grader — but type 1 is what takes a model from 20% to 80%. Structural consequence: a dataset is only valuable as far as the frontier has moved, so the only durable supply is a live business you partner with, not a dead startup's codebase. "The dirty secret of the industry is that everybody sells type 2 and bills it as type 1" |
| `el-verifiers-law` | Verifier's Law (Jason Wei) | concept | training | "The ease of training a model to do a task is proportional to how verifiable the task is." | `el-verifiability-axes` | Three axes of verifiability | concept | training | Cai's decomposition of Verifier's Law into a classifier you can apply to any profession's tasks: **asymmetry of verification** (how hard is it to decompose the task into checkable steps), **veracity of verification** (how much consensus exists about what "correct" even means), and **proliferation of verification** (how often does the real world hand you fresh examples of verified work). | `how-classify-domain-verifiability` | Read which domain matures next, with a three-step checklist | (1) Classify the profession's tasks on the three axes — asymmetry (can it be decomposed into checkable steps), veracity (is there consensus on what correct means), proliferation (does the world hand you fresh verified examples). (2) Apply the long-horizon bar labs actually use, and note vendors define it inconsistently: enforced step length, heterogeneous tool calls that aren't interchangeable, state transitions that genuinely constrain future actions, and mandatory failure recovery.
