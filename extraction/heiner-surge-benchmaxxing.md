## Review notes

1. **⚑ The Cai/Surge tension — the reason this file matters (`co-surge-ai` reused, per instructions).** Batch 13's `cai-state-of-data.md` alleged, from the analyst side, that "most benchmarks you see are quietly fake" because the dominant type-2 recipe is *sell a lab the data, then sell it the benchmark that data hill-climbs* — "Goodhart's law with a profit motive" — and named Surge among the vertically integrated expert-data giants. Heiner is a Surge insider on the same topic six days later. Reading the two against each other:
   - **Confirms, strongly, at the phenomenon level.** Benchmarks routinely diverge from real value; the most-cited ones are among the worst built; contamination is "the default outcome unless you are very, very good"; labs actively game (vote brigading with watermark signalling, undisclosed eval conditions, 27 silent Meta runs); "we as an industry aren't really in the habit of doing those disclosures". A vendor-side witness corroborating an analyst-side accusation is the strongest form this corpus collects.
   - **Complicates the mechanism.** Cai locates the corruption in a *vendor conflict of interest*. Heiner locates it in (a) construction cost — correct benchmarks cost $15M+ and $5M/yr, so builders cut corners; (b) lab-side marketing/political incentives; and (c) a demand-side inability to judge benchmark quality. He never mentions the sell-both-sides conflict at all. Note what that non-mention means structurally: **Surge sells expert data *and* publishes a benchmark (Hemingway Bench), which is exactly the structure Cai flags** — and Heiner's answer to the quality problem is "pay for the best human experts", which is also the pitch for Surge's product and its open roles. This talk is simultaneously the most detailed insider confirmation and an instance of the thing being alleged. Both facts belong in the graph; neither cancels the other.
   - **Contradicts on prognosis, not on facts.** Heiner's opening answers are "incentives, poor methodologies, **no** [not intrinsic to all benchmarks], and **yes** [we will know which models are best]". Cai's picture is a permanent fog of war. That optimism is genuine counterweight and it is the one place the two talks disagree. **If `pat-benchmark-trust-crisis` is coined as a `challenge`, a `ContradictsPattern` edge from a Heiner signal is defensible** — but I did not manufacture one, because every signal here also independently *forms* `pat-verification-gap`, and the disagreement is about whether the problem is fixable rather than about whether it exists. My recommendation: capture the disagreement in the pattern's `evolution` field rather than as a counter-edge.
2. **`pat-benchmark-trust-crisis` — new evidence on all four existing legs, plus a proposed fifth (NOT coined, per instructions).**
   - *Gaming leg*: the most concrete first-person account in the corpus of **how** labs benchmax — hire a crowdsourced army in an unfiltered arena and watermark your outputs so they know whom to vote for; run unrepresentative conditions without disclosure; test 27 models silently (Meta, from a published paper). This is a step beyond Han's reward hacking and Horthy's gameable rewards: those are models exploiting verifiers, this is *organizations* exploiting leaderboards.
   - *Contamination leg*: Opus verbatim-completes SWE-bench Verified prompts and answers; Surge measured memorization of Verified content against the rest of the same repos; the model card doesn't disclose it and no norm requires it. Joins Shi/Datacurve's git-log rates as the second measured contamination result, and adds the **non-disclosure** claim, which is new.
   - *Methodology leg*: three named benchmarks dissected — IFEval (unsolvable tasks, unverified prompts, a Cyrillic-homoglyph full score), APEX (rubric contradicts the given files; synthetic data breeding eval awareness), AutomationBench (string-match verifier collapsing Haiku and Fable to the same 20%). Plus the saturation argument: "80% and saturated" may mean "20% of tasks are broken, and the bias in which ones distorts the ranking".
   - *Economic-motive leg*: **complicated, not confirmed** — see note 1. Heiner supplies a *different* economic mechanism: the $15M/$5M cost wall as the reason correct construction doesn't happen. That is an economics-of-supply argument, where Cai's is an economics-of-conflict argument. Both are economic; they are not the same claim.
   - **⚑ Proposed FIFTH leg — demand-side illegibility.** "If you don't have the ability to assess if a benchmark is good, what you do have is the ability to assess what's popular." This is the mechanism that makes the other four legs *stable*: insider disclosure, expert dissent and public dunking do not dislodge a dominant benchmark, because the audience selecting benchmarks cannot evaluate them and therefore selects on incumbency and marketing. Every other leg describes how measurement gets corrupted; this one describes why the corruption doesn't get corrected. If the pattern is coined, I'd argue this belongs in the brief.
